🛠 **Work during class:** Figma AI (First Draft / Make Designs)

## Warm Up

**Group — Name that principle**
Look at two contrasting screenshots on the board (one cluttered, one clean). As a group, call out which design principle — contrast, alignment, repetition, or proximity — explains *why* the clean one reads better. Write your group's four one-sentence definitions on the board.

## Generate

**Individual — Prompt to variations**
Pick one brief: a landing page hero for a fictional product, or a mobile screen for a simple app idea (you choose the concept). Write one text prompt and use Figma AI to generate **3–4 distinct variations** from it. Don't hand-edit yet — just generate and collect the raw output side by side on one page.

## Critique

**Pair — Principle-based scoring**
Swap screens with a partner. For each of their variations, score it 1–3 on each of the four CRAP principles and leave one comment per score explaining *why* (e.g. "2/3 alignment — the button and heading don't share an edge with anything else on the page"). No comments like "looks good" or "I don't like it" — every comment must name a principle.

**Group — Which one wins, and why**
Back in your group, each person presents their partner's highest- and lowest-scoring variation. Discuss: did the AI's "best-looking" output actually score well on the principles, or did something more subtle win? Where did the tool clearly fail at basic design fundamentals?

> 🛠 **Next class:** Bring your favorite variation, hand-refined based on today's critique.

## Discussion

- Speed vs. intentionality: generating four variations took seconds — does that change how carefully you evaluate them, compared to sketching four options by hand?
- Where did Figma AI produce something a human designer probably wouldn't have tried? Was it good or just different?
- If a client asked you to "just use the AI version," what would you tell them about the tradeoffs?
