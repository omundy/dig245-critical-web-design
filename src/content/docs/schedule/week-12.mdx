> These two sessions are placed intentionally *after* Ch 7 (ethics) and *before* Ch 8 (generative design). By now students can read and write HTML/CSS/JS, have used Bootstrap, built forms, tested for usability, and thought critically about design ethics. They have the technical foundation to evaluate AI output and the critical vocabulary to question it.

### 12-1 Gen AI — What Is It and Who Controls It?

### Learning objectives

{/* By the end of this session you will be able to: */}
1. Distinguish AI-generated web designs from human-designed ones and articulate what visual or structural patterns give them away.
2. Map the ownership, training data, cost, and access conditions for at least 3 AI tools relevant to web design.
3. Analyze the labor and intellectual property implications of AI training data, including whose creative work is used and under what conditions.

*No book reading — instructor-created session*

**Group — AI Output Audit**
Each team is given a set of AI-generated web designs (screenshots) and a set of human-designed equivalents. Without knowing which is which: sort them into two piles. Reveal the labels. Discussion: What visual or structural tells gave it away? What didn't? What does this say about what AI "knows" about design?

**Individual — AI Landscape Map**
Research and map the AI tools relevant to web design and coding (ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, Midjourney, Figma AI, v0.dev, etc.). For each: Who made it? Who owns the training data? What does it cost? Who has access? What does it refuse to do? Document in a shared class doc.

**Discussion:** What is the labor behind AI? Who collected the training data, and under what conditions? Whose creative work was used to train these models?

### 12-2 Gen AI — AI as a Tool

### Learning objectives

{/* By the end of this session you will be able to: */}
1. Use an AI assistant to diagnose a real bug in your own code, documenting the prompt, suggestion, and whether you understood the fix.
2. Evaluate when AI assistance genuinely supports learning vs. when it substitutes for understanding you need to develop yourself.
3. Contribute to a class AI policy that distinguishes legitimate uses from uses that undermine learning, voice, or critical intent.

*No book reading — instructor-created session*

**Individual — AI-Assisted Debug Session**
Take a broken piece of your own code from a previous project. Use an AI assistant (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot) to help diagnose it. Document: What prompt did you use? What did the AI suggest? Was it correct? Did you understand why? What would have happened if you'd just pasted it in without understanding it?

**Group — Class AI Policy Workshop**
Draft the class's working AI policy for the rest of the semester. Consider: What tasks does AI help with legitimately? What does it undermine (your learning, your voice, the critical intent of a project)? What do you need to disclose? What's the difference between using AI to debug a for-loop vs. using it to generate your critical intent statement?

### 12-3 Generative Design — Context + Survey

### Learning objectives

{/* By the end of this session you will be able to: */}
1. Analyze 5 works of generative design by identifying what inputs generate the output, what is random, and what is rule-based.
2. Articulate the difference between AI-generated and algorithmically generated design using vocabulary from Ch 8 Context.
3. Write a plain-English visual algorithm using only rules and randomness, then implement a peer's description in code.

📖 **Read before class:** Ch 8 Context, pp. 253–261
🛠 **Work during class:** Module 8.1 *A Survey of Generative Practices* (pp. 262–268)

**Individual — Generative Art Survey**
Following Module 8.1: find 5 examples of generative design or art ([CWD Index](https://criticalwebdesign.github.io/index/), [Whitney Artport](https://whitney.org/artport), or your own search). For each: What inputs generate the output? What is random? What is rule-based? What is the artist's role vs. the algorithm's?

**Group — "Write an Algorithm" Challenge**
Each person writes a plain-English description of a visual scene using only rules and randomness. Teams swap and attempt to code each other's description. How faithful is the implementation to the intent?
